Artwork presentation

Corazones Distantes II

Artist: Nataha Barria Cuella (Natt)
Year 2026
Semester Second semester
Date November 25, 2026
License CC BY-SA 4.0

Second part of the series ‘Distant Hearts’. In times when we surrender ourselves more and more to technology, without weighing the consequences, without knowing how it affects us or in how many ways. Going against the current of its inert nature, and with its contemporaneity increasingly uprooted from the human, this piece asks: Can a few lines of code give us back some of the feeling we lost? Can distance break that certainty the heart cannot explain? Or has depth lost the battle against hazy, accelerated ambiguity?

Technical, aesthetic, and conceptual development

“The mediation of images imposes a distance, and distance creates a space in which words can resonate and multiply their expression beyond the utilitarian.” AIRA (2016).

Images. Distance. Space. Expressions beyond the utilitarian. Probably the best synthesis of the work lies in those few words. Fortunately, the purpose of this text is not to compress or summarize. On the contrary, it is driven by the desire to expand and deepen the ideas explored around code.

The decline of the social, institutional and interpersonal factions we are going through in this accelerationist present, which at times smells of dystopia, is nothing new. Even so, I feel compelled to point out and question the effects it has on what is, without fear of exaggerating, the most human and irrevocable dimension of all: personal bonds, Love, if you want to call it that. And more difficult than meeting someone to love or to open up to, the task of caring for that bond so that it endures has become tormenting. Partly because of the individual notion of algorithmized bubbles, of expectations idealized into standards only attainable in the digital world (clearly, due to its filtered and artificial reality, which we buy without blinking). And largely because the effects of that double identity <the tangible and the virtual> end up, in most cases, scattering, confusing and draining the will of one’s own desire.

“Program code is characterized by the fact that here ‘saying’ coincides with ‘doing’. […] This ‘coded performativity’ has immediate political consequences in the real and virtual spaces […] (among others, on the Internet) in which we increasingly move and live: ultimately, this means that this coded performativity mobilizes or immobilizes its users. In this way, code becomes law.” ARNS (2005).

Because we live so many lives at once that we no longer even know which one we truly want. Because we consume far more than we think, far beyond even what we create, and we don’t even choose for ourselves what we consume; we relinquish even that. How could we not be dissociated? Fragmented, divided, switched off. It saddens me to see how fast mental, bodily and sensory overstimulation is growing. A trend completely opposite to the way care is collapsing: care for feeling, for loving, for connecting from the heart and not through a few pixels. In this contemporary moment, what is left for art, and specifically for digital art, but to entrench itself in the much-needed search for sensitivity, or to surrender to the avalanche of heartless hyperconnectivity, devoid of reason?

As for me, I will choose love a thousand and one times. Even if I must beg for tenderness or burn with emotion, I will lay bare every last inner pain, so long as I neither yield nor bend, not even slightly, the most transcendent dimension: my expression.

Bibliography

AIRA, César. ([2013], 2016). “Sobre el arte contemporáneo”. ARNS, Inke. (2005). “El código como acto de habla performativo”

Brief resume

Natasha Barria is a multimedia artist, composer, musical performer, and producer of cultural and audiovisual events. Born in Río Gallegos, Santa Cruz, she moved to Buenos Aires at the age of 18 to begin her professional career, her first step being to enroll in the Bachelor’s Degree in Multimedia Arts at UNA (National University of the Arts). Shaped by the city’s sociocultural dynamics, the euphoric volatility of the changes in its ways of living, and the intellectual, emotional and spiritual transformation she was almost forced to undergo in order to adapt to her new surroundings, she is forging her path in the field independently, drawing support from the independent community of Greater Buenos Aires’ western suburbs. Her main stylistic pursuit, across the various formats in which she presents her work, lies in the expression of vulnerability and sensitivity, as a way of replicating some essential part of her experiences, in the hope that someone else might find in her work a message that soothes or accompanies their own.

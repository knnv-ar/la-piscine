Simpoiesis

Character movement using arrow keys: Up, down, left, and right.

While interacting with your character through basic keyboard controls, the scene initially appears static. At a specific location, a “mechanism” is triggered that breaks this state of immobility. By filling the single empty space within a given round, you activate this “mechanism.” Movement is only possible in this way; if you move away from this specific spot on the canvas, the entire system comes to a halt.

Technical, Aesthetic, and Conceptual Development

This new-media art project (.1) originated from a single impulse: to find the antonym of “autopoiesis”—a term mentioned by a professor during a class. This led me to Donna Jeanne Haraway, and to her remarkable body of work and career. I discovered her book, Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene (2016) (.2). In it, she re-examines our relationship with nature, the irreversible damage inflicted upon the environment, and the problematic nature of heteronormative logic and the nuclear family as a social model for inhabiting the Earth. In Chapter 3, she explores, argues for, and defines the concept of “sympoiesis.” To quote her own work:

“Sympoiesis is a simple word; it means ‘making-with.’ Nothing makes itself; nothing is truly autopoietic or self-organizing.” (Haraway, 2016)

With the meaning of this term in mind, I realized that the paradigm for my creative process had been set. From the outset, I envisioned an arrangement of characters in a circle, and the search for a rotational effect followed almost immediately; the idea of ​​gears came later. Visually, the works of Jared Tarbell—specifically “Sand Dollar” (.3) and “Invader Fractal” (.4)—proved highly useful for my creative process. Even so, I felt that the concept of sympoiesis had not been synthesized to the standard I required. I therefore turned to José Luis Brea’s “Redefinition of Artistic Practices” (Redefinición de las prácticas artísticas) to construct a more robust critique of the fallacy of autopoiesis. As Brea (2008) observes:

“Social mechanisms of recognition and differentiation—of socialization and subjectivization, of belonging to a social group and distinguishing oneself within it—rest, above all, upon aestheticized value. It is this specific weight—which the new capitalism imparts to objects and relationships, whether material or immaterial—that determines their new social value. This is all the more true in a globalized world, where the circulation of goods, forms, and commodities transcends any specific GEO-BIO-POLITICAL boundary or context of identification. In this globalized world, characterized by the loss of clear markers of identity, the need to implement mechanisms for the production of identity and differentiation grows exponentially.” (.5)

By conceiving and grasping the problems inherent in the discourse of autonomy and individualism—while reaffirming the concept of the “Chthulucene” (a term coined by Donna Haraway, which stands in contrast to the more commonly used “Anthropocene” or “Capitalocene”—terms that both Brea and Haraway critique for their systematic deployment)—I arrived at my final decision: to create a work that is, in principle, static. This choice underscores the idea that “partial union” is merely a fantasy—one that sustains nothing more than isolated circles, failing to function as a cohesive whole. My reasoning is linked to the very nature of the act of production, which unfolded in a sympoietic manner—given that the knowledge, the infrastructure, and the technology upon which we rely as producers are, by no means, either wholly or even partially our own.
